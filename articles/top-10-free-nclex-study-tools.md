## Detailed Reviews

### 1. RegisteredNurseRN — Best Free Fundamentals Review

RegisteredNurseRN is one of the most useful free resources for rebuilding nursing-school fundamentals. It can help with procedures, disease processes, lab interpretation, medication categories, maternal-newborn review, cardiac topics, endocrine disorders, and common med-surg concepts.

Use it when you realize you are missing questions because you do not understand the underlying content. Watch or read one focused topic, then immediately answer related questions. That keeps the resource active instead of turning your study session into passive review.

The limitation is readiness measurement. Free topic videos do not usually simulate a mixed NCLEX exam or full Next Gen case set.

### 2. Nurselabs — Best Free Supplemental Quiz Library

Nurselabs can be helpful when you need extra exposure to a topic. Its quizzes and review pages make it easy to practice fundamentals, pharmacology, maternity, pediatrics, and med-surg concepts without paying for another tool.

Treat these quizzes as supplemental practice, not final readiness proof. Topic quizzes can improve recall, but the NCLEX often mixes categories and asks you to choose the safest action under pressure. Keep an error log that explains why you missed each item: content gap, prioritization error, misread stem, missed safety clue, or weak delegation logic.

### 3. SimpleNursing Free Videos — Best for Mnemonics

SimpleNursing is popular because it turns complex topics into memorable explanations. Free videos can be especially useful for pharmacology, endocrine, cardiac, renal, electrolyte, and disease-process review.

The risk is over-watching. Mnemonics help you remember facts, but the NCLEX asks you to apply facts to patient scenarios. Use a 1:2 rule: for every 20 minutes of video, spend 40 minutes answering questions and reviewing rationales.

### 4. Official NCSBN NCLEX Information — Best for Exam Orientation

Official NCSBN resources help you understand the exam format, Next Gen item types, clinical judgment model, and what the NCLEX is designed to measure. This is not a giant question bank, but it is still essential because it keeps your prep aligned with the real exam.

Review official item-type descriptions before you start heavy practice. If you understand matrix, bow-tie, case study, and extended multiple response formats, practice questions feel less intimidating.

### 5. School ATI, HESI, or Elsevier Access — Best If Already Included

Many students already have ATI, HESI, Elsevier, or similar resources through their nursing program. If you have paid for access through tuition or fees, use it before buying anything else.

The challenge is organization. School platforms can feel overwhelming, especially after graduation. Create a simple weekly routine: mixed questions, weak-topic review, NGN practice, remediation, and one readiness-style check. If your school tool lacks clear NGN rationales or enough mixed practice, add free resources or a low-cost question bank to fill the gap.

### 6. YouTube NCLEX Review Channels — Best for Quick Topic Refreshers

YouTube can help you quickly review topics like prioritization, delegation, cardiac rhythms, endocrine emergencies, maternity complications, pediatric milestones, and pharmacology classes.

Choose videos intentionally. Search one weakness, watch one or two focused videos, and stop. Do not let the algorithm build your study plan. Your missed questions and rationales should decide what you watch next.

### 7. Free Flashcards and Quizlet-Style Decks — Best for Recall

Flashcards can support lab values, isolation precautions, antidotes, therapeutic ranges, cranial nerves, immunizations, developmental milestones, and high-yield medications. They are good for recall, especially during short study windows.

Flashcards are not enough for readiness because they rarely test clinical judgment in realistic patient scenarios. Use them as warmups or quick review, then move into application questions.

### 8. Library and Nursing School Resource Pages — Best Hidden Free Access

Some students can access NCLEX books, test-prep databases, or study guides through a public library, university library, or nursing school resource page. This is an underrated free option, especially if you need structured review material but cannot pay for another subscription.

Ask your library whether it provides test-prep databases, ebook access, or board-review materials. Even if the content is not perfect, it can support weak-topic review and reduce the number of paid tools you need.

### 9. Free Study Planners and Error Logs — Best for Structure

A free planner may not teach content, but it can prevent wasted time. A strong NCLEX planner includes daily mixed questions, topic review, NGN case practice, remediation, rest, and weekly checkpoints.

The most important free document is an error log. Track the question topic, why you missed it, the correct safety principle, and what you will do differently next time. Patterns in the error log are more useful than a single daily score.

## Sample Free-First 30-Day NCLEX Plan

A simple free-first NCLEX plan can look like this:

- **Days 1-3:** Take a baseline mixed quiz from available resources and identify the top five weak categories.
- **Days 4-10:** Review one weak topic daily using free videos or notes, then complete related practice questions.
- **Days 11-17:** Increase mixed questions, add prioritization and delegation practice, and update your error log.
- **Days 18-24:** Add NGN case-style practice where available; if free resources feel thin, consider a low-cost NGN bank.
- **Days 25-28:** Focus on readiness checks, missed rationales, safety principles, and high-risk topics.
- **Days 29-30:** Light review, test-day planning, sleep, and confidence building.

The goal is not to study everything equally. The goal is to find the mistakes that could cost you points on exam day and correct them before they repeat.
